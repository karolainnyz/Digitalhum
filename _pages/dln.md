<h1 style="color: hotpink; font-weight: bold; font-family: 'Times New Roman', serif;">*Final Digital Literacy Reflection</h1>

I am a 19-year-old university student majoring in Hospitality and minoring in Nutrition. Before entering college, I mostly associated digital literacy with knowing how to use to social media, searching for information online, or just completing assignments on my computer. However, throughout my time taking the Intro to Digital Humanities course in Abu Dhabi, I have started to understand that digital literacy is much more than just basic technological skills. Digital literacy invovles communication, organization, collaboration, critical thinking, and learning how to responisbly use digital tools in ways that positively affect other people. 

As the hospitality industry evolves, it is inevitable that technology plays a major role in everyday work. Many parts of the industry depend on digital systems, whether it is event planning, customer communication, menu design, scheduling, or even digital marketing. Because of this, I use digital tools constantly throughout my day! Programs like Google Docs, Google Drive, Canva, Excel, and PowerPoint have become essential for organizing my coursework and completing projects. During class or even in my extracurriculars, we often rely on Google Docs when working in groups because it allows everyone to contribute ideas in real time, leave comments, and edit together without confusion. Google Drive also helps me stay organized since I can separate assignments, nutrition notes, and hospitality presentations into folders that are easy to access from anywhere.

One area where I feel especially comfortable is content creation and visual communication. Hospitality is a field that depends heavily on presentation and customer experience, so learning how to create visually appealing digital content has become an important skill for me. I use Canva frequently to create menus, promotional graphics, presentations, and social media content. I enjoy experimenting with layouts, colors, and images because visuals can influence how people feel about a restaurant, event, or product. Through this process, I have also become more aware of accessibility and communication. I try to use clear fonts, readable designs, captions, and simple language so that information is easier for different audiences to understand.

Social media has also shaped my understanding of digital literacy. Platforms like Instagram, TikTok, and Pinterest are not only spaces for entertainment, but also tools for branding, communication, and creativity. In hospitality, social media presence can strongly influence customer engagement and business success. I often pay attention to how restaurants, hotels, and food brands market themselves online through visuals, short videos, and customer interaction. Watching how businesses create online identities has helped me understand the importance of tone, audience awareness, and digital professionalism. At the same time, it has taught me to think more critically about what people choose to present online and how digital spaces can shape public perception.

Another important aspect of this course was learning how digital information is organized and annotated. Before this semester, I never really thought about how place names, historical references, or geographic information are digitally categorized behind the scenes. While using Recogito in class and learning about gazetteers, I realized how important annotation and metadata are for making digital humanities projects searchable, interactive, and meaningful. During our class activity annotating in Recogito. I saw how locations and historical references could be digitally encoded and connected to broader geographic systems. This helped me understand that digital literacy also involves structuring information in ways that make it accessible and useful for other people.

Because of my nutrition minor, I have also started engaging more with digital research and data organization. When researching nutrition topics, I use online academic databases, Google Scholar, and reference management tools to collect sources and organize information. I have learned how important it is to evaluate the reliability of digital information, especially in areas like nutrition where misinformation spreads quickly online. Social media often promotes unrealistic diets, misleading health advice, or unsupported claims, so I have become more careful about checking sources, comparing information, and identifying credible research. This has helped me strengthen my critical thinking skills and become more responsible when sharing information with others.

Another important idea I learned in this course was that maps and spatial visualizations are not neutral. When we explored projects like smell maps, music topography, and UFO sighting datasets using tools such as Kepler.gl and OpenStreetMap, I began to understand how spatial humanities can reveal patterns connected to culture, memory, and human behavior. Before this course, I mainly thought of maps as tools for navigation. Now I see them as a way of asking humanities-based questions through data. Digital maps can also tell stories about people and communities. By looking at patterns in location data, movement, or even things like smells, music venues, or streets named after women, we can learn more about the experiences, histories, and identities of different groups of people. In hospitality, spatial thinking could help analyze tourism patterns, restaurant accessibility, sourcing locations, or customer movement through spaces. This made me realize that digital literacy also includes learning how to interpret visualized data critically rather than simply accepting it at face value.

Although I am not highly experienced in coding or advanced technology, this course has introduced me to new perspectives on digital tools and computational thinking! Before, I saw coding and data analysis as subjects mainly connected to Computer Science majors. However, I now understand that digital methods can also support hospitality,  nutrition, and any other major in practical ways. For example, technology can help businesses track customer preferences, organize general costs, analyze surveys, or improve efficiency in event planning. Learning about these possibilities has made technology feel more approachable and relevant to my own field of study.

At the same time, learning about artificial intelligence and digital automation has raised important ethical questions for me. AI tools can help generate ideas, organize information, and improve efficiency, but they also have limitations. In hospitality and nutrition, human interaction and personal judgment are important. Technology may assist people, but it should not completely replace the human side of these professions. I believe it is important to balance innovation with empathy and responsibility, especially when dealing with people’s health, experiences, and personal information.

One of the most interesting parts of the course for me was learning about images as data and the role of machine learning in image analysis. We discussed concepts such as image classification, clustering, computer vision, image embeddings, and multimodal AI. At first, these ideas sounded very technical and distant from my major. However, I started realizing how these systems already influence hospitality and food industries through recommendation algorithms, digital advertising, visual branding, and customer analytics. Learning about projects like ImageNet and Distant Viewing Explorer helped me understand that computers do not “see” images the same way humans do. Instead, they rely on labels, annotations, and patterns created through datasets. This raised important questions for me about bias, representation, and accuracy in AI systems. The reading from Distant Viewing especially stood out to me when it explained that digital image analysis depends on annotations and structured labels. That idea made me realize how much human decision-making shapes supposedly “automatic” systems.

Another concept that influenced my understanding of digital literacy was the idea of building and analyzing a digital corpus. During the semester, we discussed how large collections of images, texts, or datasets can be organized and studied to identify patterns that may not be immediately visible to humans. Learning about clustering versus classification in Orange Data Mining helped me understand the difference between allowing data to organize itself and training systems through human labeling. Even though these concepts initially seemed very technical, I began connecting them to hospitality and marketing. For example, businesses constantly analyze customer reviews, food trends, images, and online interactions to better understand consumer behavior. This made me realize that digital literacy is also about understanding how information is collected, categorized, and interpreted by both humans and machines. It also showed me how much human decisions still shape digital systems, even when technology appears to operate automatically.

Reflecting on my own strengths, I believe I am adaptable and willing to learn new digital tools quickly. I am confident when it comes to communication, organization, visual presentation, and collaborative work. However, I also recognize areas where I still need improvement. I would like to become more comfortable with data analysis, spreadsheets, and basic technical skills that could help me better understand trends and organize information. I also want to continue improving my ability to critically evaluate digital information and use technology in ethical and responsible ways.

Overall, this course has changed how I think about digital literacy. I no longer see it as simply knowing how to use technology. Instead, I see it as a combination of technical, social, and critical skills that influence how people communicate, collaborate, and solve problems in the modern world. By the end of the course, my understanding became much broader and more critical. I now think about how data is collected, labeled, visualized, and interpreted. I also think more carefully about the ethical side of digital tools, especially AI systems and decision-making. This course showed me that digital literacy is not only about technical ability, but also about understanding the social, cultural, and human consequences of technology. As I continue studying hospitality and nutrition, I hope to strengthen these skills and learn how to use digital tools in ways that improve both efficiency and human connection. For me, digital literacy is ultimately about using technology thoughtfully while still keeping people at the center of the experience.
